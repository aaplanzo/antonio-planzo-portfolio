The thesis

The prize is real. The edge is in who ships to factories first.

Humanoid robotics is the largest opportunity in ARK's Autonomous Technology and Robotics strategy. In 2026, though, the investable edge sits with industrial-first private humanoid makers and the component suppliers every one of them needs. Factory payback already works on paper; the consumer timeline keeps slipping.

Claim 1

The market is large

ARK sizes humanoid robotics as a potential $26 trillion opportunity. Elon Musk told investors in April 2026 that Optimus will be Tesla's biggest product.

Claim 2

Timelines are slipping

Tesla targeted several thousand Optimus units in 2025. Its July 2026 shareholder update said production had still not started, so value shifts to whoever ships to factories first.

Claim 3

Industrial buyers adopt first

At Musk's $20,000–$30,000 target price, a robot doing half a U.S. worker's output pays back in under a year. Capability, not price, is the gate.

For ARK: weight private-market sourcing toward humanoid makers with signed industrial pilots, and toward the actuator, AI and rare-earth supply chain that every maker needs regardless of who wins.

Unit economics

How fast does a humanoid pay for itself?

Payback equals robot price divided by the labor it replaces. ARK's benchmark is the BLS employer cost of $46.21 an hour, over 40-hour weeks and 50-week years: $92,420 a year. Move the sliders.

Musk's long-run target is $20,000–$30,000. McKinsey puts typical humanoid bills of materials at $30,000–$150,000 today.

The model leaves out maintenance, power, software fees and integration, so real payback runs longer. Even at $100,000, a robot matching one worker recovers its cost in about a year, so price is not the binding constraint. Reliable task capability is.

The value chain

Prioritize the joints and the brain.

Actuators are the largest cost pool and the tightest supply bottleneck. Robot AI and training data are the real gate on adoption. Both earn returns whichever humanoid maker wins. Scroll through the robot.

Priority: first

Actuators and the precision drivetrain

The motors, harmonic drives and planetary roller screws that move every joint. Inside each actuator, the gearbox alone is 30–50% of the cost, and Tesla's Gen 3 Optimus hands carry about 50 actuators against 28 in the rest of the body.

This is where Wright's Law cost declines will be won or lost, since bills of materials must fall from $30,000+ to under $20,000.

Supply risk
High. China makes ~35% of motors and ~40% of precision bearings.

Priority: first

Robot AI models and training data

The payback model shows a robot pays for itself once it can actually do the work, so capability is the gate and data is the scarce input. NVIDIA generated 780,000 synthetic trajectories in 11 hours, equal to about 6,500 hours of human demonstrations. Mixing synthetic with real data improved its GR00T N1 humanoid model by 40%.

Tesla's first Optimus builds go to data collection, and its Cortex 2 cluster trains both vehicle and humanoid autonomy.

Rare-earth permanent magnets

Every motor in every joint depends on them. Rare-earth export controls already delayed Optimus motor production once. McKinsey estimates an Optimus Gen 2 bill of materials at about $46,000 with Chinese suppliers versus about $131,000 without.

Very high. China processes ~90% of permanent magnets and mines ~69% of rare earths.

Risks

The main risk is time, not market size.

Capability gap

ARK puts humanoids at 200,000 times the development complexity of robotaxis, and some public Optimus demos relied on remote teleoperation.

Manufacturing and cash burn

Tesla guides 2026 capex above $25B with negative free cash flow, and Musk says Optimus needs a totally new supply chain.

Supply chain exposure

Rare-earth export controls already delayed Optimus motors once.

Price compression

HSBC sees Chinese humanoid commercialization moving faster than overseas peers, and K-Scale Labs priced a bot at $9,000.

Private valuations

Round prices like Figure's reported ~$40B assume success well before revenue.

What would change this thesis

  • No humanoid maker shipping more than 10,000 units a year to outside customers by end of 2028, the start of Wood's window
  • Industrial pilots at Mercedes, GXO, Airbus or Hyundai not converting to fleet orders
  • Real-world payback, including maintenance and integration, running above three years
  • Chinese makers reaching sub-$10,000 prices with comparable capability
