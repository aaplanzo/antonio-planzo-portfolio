The report

Output more than doubled. One new plant made nothing usable.

The Department of War set a goal of 100,000 rounds of 155 mm artillery ammunition a month by Oct. 1, 2025. The Inspector General evaluated how close the Army got, and why it fell short.

Every round is built in two stages. A metal parts plant makes the shell body. A second plant fills it with explosives, assembles it and packs it. The first stage is the bottleneck, and most of that gap traces to one contractor-run plant in Mesquite, Texas.

For immediate release, July 9, 2026

Army more than doubled 155 mm artillery output but missed its 100,000-round goal, evaluation finds

A new plant in Texas has not produced a part that meets specifications after $469 million in spending.

ALEXANDRIA, Va.1 — The Department of War Office of Inspector General on July 93 released an evaluation finding that the Army raised monthly production of 155 mm2 artillery ammunition from 14,000 to 36,000 rounds but did not reach its goal of 100,000 rounds a month by Oct. 1, 20253.

Most of the shortfall traces to a contractor-owned, contractor-operated plant in Mesquite, Texas, that the Army planned to supply 30,000 shell bodies a month. As of March 2026, the plant had not produced a single part that met contract specifications. The Army spent $469 million4 to establish the plant from December 2022 through December 2025.

Without Mesquite, the Army's three other shell-body plants are projected to support 71,000 rounds a month, or 71%4 of the goal.

The evaluation found that Army acquisition officials6 accepted the risk of a contractor plan to buy and adapt equipment built for an older shell, the M107, to make the newer M795. The equipment had not been proven to meet U.S. specifications.

The Inspector General made three recommendations. The Army should find and carry out a way to produce the 29,000 additional shell bodies a month it still needs; review how the $469 million was spent and whether any of it can be recovered; and determine whether the Mesquite contract was properly issued under an existing contract for the Scranton Army Ammunition Plant.

Army officials agreed with all three recommendations. The Inspector General considers two resolved and one unresolved, because the Army's production plan did not say how or when the new sources would reach the required rate.

  1. Dateline. City in capitals plus the AP state abbreviation, "Va."
  2. "155 mm." AP uses "mm" with a space for weapons and no hyphen. The report writes "155-mm."
  3. Dates. Month abbreviated with a specific date ("Oct. 1"); year dropped when it's the current year.
  4. Numbers. Numerals for 10 and up, "$469 million," and the % sign.
  5. Attribution. "Said," never "stated" or "noted."
  6. No acronym soup. "Capability Program Executive Ammunition & Energetics" becomes "Army acquisition officials."

The lede leads with progress and the miss in one sentence, so the release is accurate even if a reader stops there.

Did the Army waste $469 million?Tough

AnswerThe evaluation found the Army spent $469 million to set up the Mesquite plant and that, as of March 2026, it had not produced a part meeting specifications. We said that money could have gone to other Army priorities, and we recommended the Army review how it was spent and whether any of it can be recovered.

BridgeThe more important point for readiness is the 29,000 shell bodies a month the Army still needs, and that's what our first recommendation addresses.

Don'tUse the word "waste" or characterize the money as lost. The recovery review is still open.

Who is the contractor?Tough

AnswerThe finding refers to "the contractor." The Army's written comments, published with the report, identify the company it reached a settlement with. I'd refer you to those comments and to the Army for anything about the company.

Don'tCharacterize the company's performance beyond what the report says. The contractor reviewed the report, and a footnote records its position that its performance met or exceeded requirements.

Why wasn't the work competed?Tough

AnswerThe Army issued the Mesquite work as task orders under an existing property management contract for the Scranton Army Ammunition Plant. Plant officials raised concerns that it wasn't opened to competition, so we recommended the Army determine whether it was issued properly. The Army reviewed it and says it was within scope. That recommendation stays open until we receive the documentation.

Is the money coming back?Tough

AnswerThe Army told us it negotiated a confidential settlement in April 2026 and realigned production to northeastern Pennsylvania at a lower price and higher quantity. We consider that recommendation resolved but open until we see documentation of the review and the planned recovery of funds.

Don'tDiscuss settlement terms. They're confidential; refer questions to the Army.

Why do you call one recommendation unresolved if the Army agreed?Tough

AnswerAgreeing isn't the same as a plan. The Army said it will produce 20,000 shell bodies a month at Mesquite and 9,000 from other industrial base sources by December 2028, but it didn't explain how a plant that couldn't make the part as of March 2026 will get there. When it gives us those details, we can resolve it.

Did you find fraud or wrongdoing?

AnswerNo. This was an evaluation of production capability. It found that officials accepted a known risk that didn't pay off. It doesn't allege fraud.

Don'tSpeculate about other investigations, open or closed.

What does this mean for readiness, and for Ukraine?

AnswerWe found that falling short of 100,000 rounds a month could reduce readiness and raise the risk of not meeting U.S., allied and partner needs in a future conflict. For context, the report notes the Department's 155 mm inventory dropped by more than 3.6 million rounds over four years, mostly through presidential drawdowns to Ukraine.

Don'tComment on current stockpile levels. This report doesn't assess them; refer to the Army.

When will the Army hit 100,000 a month?

AnswerThe plants that fill and pack shells are on track for 140,000 a month by December 2027. Shell bodies are the limit: existing plants are projected to reach 71,000 by September 2026, and the Army's plan for the rest runs to December 2028. For the Army's own timeline, I'd refer you to the Army.

What about 120 mm tank ammunition?

AnswerThat's part of the same evaluation project. We're issuing a separate report on it.

Don'tGive a release date or preview findings.
