Field Notes · CRS-20

By Antonio Planzo · Cape Canaveral Air Force Station · March 2020

At 11:50 p.m., an instantaneous launch window closed to zero and nine Merlin engines turned night into day over the Space Coast. From the viewing area, the light arrived first. Then the sound, a crackle you feel in your chest more than hear. Falcon 9 climbed off Space Launch Complex 40 carrying a Dragon capsule loaded with roughly 4,300 pounds of cargo and science bound for the International Space Station.

The booster under it, B1059, had already flown. Three months earlier it launched CRS-19, landed on a droneship in the Atlantic, and was refurbished for this flight. Reuse was the whole story of the night. This was also the final flight of the original Dragon spacecraft, closing out the first Commercial Resupply Services contract that began flying in 2012.

About two and a half minutes up, main engine cutoff. Stage separation four seconds later. While the second stage carried Dragon to orbit, the first stage flipped around and executed three more burns: a boostback to reverse course toward the coast, an entry burn to survive reentry, and a final landing burn.

Roughly eight minutes after leaving the pad, it was back. Landing Zone 1 sits about six miles south of SLC-40, so the booster touched down almost within sight of where it started. Sonic booms rolled across the Cape, then silence, then cheering. It was SpaceX's 50th successful booster landing, and the first return-to-launch-site landing in months. Watching a skyscraper-sized machine descend through the dark, light its engine, deploy four legs, and stop, gently, on a concrete pad is the closest thing to science fiction I have witnessed in person.
